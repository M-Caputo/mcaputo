Miranda Caputo: Research

I am currently working with Dr. Rupali Chandar at the University of Toledo/Ritter Astrophysical Research Center. My research focuses on using photometric data from JWST and HST to find and categorize stellar clusters in nearby (d<100Mpc) extreme galaxy systems. We are able to use these demographics to aid in our understanding of the creation and evaporation of clusters in extreme systems and in determining the star formation histories of these (usually) merging systems.

Before coming to Toledo, I worked at the SOFIA Science Center in NASA Ames with B-G Andersson researching the polarization of dust grains around AGB stars and in PDRs using optical spectropolarimetry and SOFIA's upGREAT and HAWC+ insturments.

Cluster Demographics

I work mainly with the Clusters, Clumps, Dust, & Gas in extreme galaxies (CCDG) survey. This sample consists of three Blue Compact Dwarfs (BCDs) and 8 Luminous InfraRed Galaxies (LIRGs) within 100 Mpcs. We utilize 6 HST photometry bands (including Hα) and JWST NIRCam photometry in some galaxies to SED fit ages and reddenings as accurately as possible using SSP models. We then determine age-distributions and cluster mass functions to obtain galaxy-wide properties, such as Gamma (% of stellar mass in clusters vs. total stellar mass) and also determine star formation histories of galaxies with variable Star Formation Rates (SFRs).

The CCDG sample is full of galaxies with high SFRs and SFR densities. These types of galaxies allow us to probe the most extreme enviroments and determine if they affect the creation or dissolution of clusters more than in spiral galaxies.

Shifting from Optical to JWST

I am P.I. on approved JWST cycle 3 proposal GO-06035 to detect embedded clusters in the extreme galaxy NGC 1614. I am also a co-I on two other JWST proposals (cycle 2 GO-03348 and cycle 4 GO-08564) to find embedded clusters and make deeper cluster catalogs in two more extreme galaxies in the CCDG sample and to look along the Phantom Void, a massive star-forming bubble in the nearby spiral galaxy NGC 628.

The main goal of these JWST projects is to test how well we understand cluster evolution at the most extreme levels we can probe in the nearby universe. How many young (age<3Mry) embedded clusters are there that we have missed with HST observations? Do these change the age-distributions and cluster mass functions in these galaxies? We are just starting to scratch the surface of these questions in this new and exciting frontier of astronomy.
